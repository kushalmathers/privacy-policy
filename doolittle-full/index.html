April eighteenth, nineteen forty-two. Sixteen American bombers rose from the Pacific on a mission that had never been attempted. This was the Doolittle Raid.

Four months after Pearl Harbor, the carrier USS Hornet steamed toward Japan. On her deck sat sixteen Army B-25 Mitchell bombers, led by Lieutenant Colonel James Doolittle. Eighty airmen had volunteered for a one-way gamble.

Then a Japanese picket boat spotted the task force. The Americans could not wait. Still roughly six hundred and fifty miles from Japan, Doolittle received the order: launch now.

With the carrier pitching in heavy seas, Doolittle released the brakes. His bomber lurched down the short deck, dropped toward the waves, then climbed. One by one, all sixteen aircraft followed.

Flying low to avoid detection, the raiders crossed the Japanese coast. They struck military and industrial targets in Tokyo, Yokohama, Nagoya, and Kobe. The attack lasted only minutes, but it shattered the illusion that the home islands were untouchable.

Without enough fuel to return, the crews pressed west. Fifteen aircraft came down in or near China; one landed in the Soviet Union. Three airmen died during bailout or landing. Eight were captured by Japan. Three were executed, and another died in captivity.

The physical damage was limited, but the impact was enormous. American morale surged, while Japan diverted forces to defend the homeland. The Doolittle Raid did not end the war. It proved that eighty volunteers could change its momentum.
